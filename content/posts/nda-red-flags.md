An NDA arrives in your inbox. You've signed dozens of them. This one looks like all the others — a few pages, standard confidentiality obligations, mutual. You sign it and move on.

Six months later, you launch a product that a former vendor claims infringes their NDA. Now you're in discovery.

NDAs are the most routinely mis-read documents in business. Here are five provisions that should give you pause before you countersign.

## 1. Definition of "Confidential Information" That's Too Broad

Most NDAs define confidential information as anything marked "confidential" or anything that "would reasonably be understood" to be confidential. That second half is where deals go wrong.

An overbroad definition — one that sweeps in all verbal communications, all information shared in any context, or all information "related to" the disclosing party's business — creates exposure for work you do independently.

**What to look for:** Language like "any information disclosed directly or indirectly" or "all information relating to disclosing party's business operations." These catch too much.

**What to ask for:** A definition limited to specifically marked written disclosures, or a carve-out for information independently developed by the receiving party.

## 2. Residuals Clauses That Strip the NDA of Meaning

A residuals clause allows the receiving party to use any information "retained in the unaided memories of individuals" who had access to the confidential information. In other words: if your engineer remembers it, you can build with it.

![Close-up of a contract clause being highlighted](/static/images/blog/contract-clause-inline.png)

This clause is standard in Big Tech vendor agreements. It's commercially reasonable for them; it's a serious risk if you're the disclosing party sharing anything of real value.

**Red flag:** Look for "residuals" or "retained in memory" or "unaided human memory" in the permitted uses section.

**Counter:** Strike the clause entirely, or limit it to generic skills and general knowledge — not specific technical implementations or business strategies.

## 3. Unilateral vs. Mutual Obligations

A mutual NDA binds both parties equally. A one-sided NDA only binds the recipient. The problem: many "mutual" NDAs have asymmetric obligations buried in the definitions.

Check whether:
- The term length is the same for both parties' disclosures
- The carve-outs (public domain, independent development, legal compulsion) apply equally
- The return/destruction of materials obligation applies to both parties

A mutual NDA where only your confidentiality period is 3 years, while the counterparty's is 1 year, is not actually mutual.

## 4. Non-Solicitation Clauses Hidden in the NDA

NDAs are confidentiality agreements. They shouldn't contain non-solicitation provisions — but they often do, tucked into Section 6 after three pages of confidentiality language no one reads carefully.

A non-solicitation clause in a vendor NDA might prevent you from hiring anyone who worked at that company for 12–24 months — even people who had no involvement in your relationship and whom you recruited independently.

**Red flag:** Any language about "employees," "contractors," "personnel," or "team members" in a document titled "Non-Disclosure Agreement."

**Counter:** Strike entirely, or limit to direct solicitation of individuals who were specifically involved in the confidential relationship.

## 5. Injunctive Relief Language That Waives Your Defenses

Most NDAs include a provision saying that breach "would cause irreparable harm" and that the disclosing party is entitled to injunctive relief "without the necessity of posting bond." This is standard — but variants of this clause can waive defenses you'd otherwise have.

Watch for language that says the receiving party "agrees that" breach would cause irreparable harm, or that the receiving party "stipulates" the appropriateness of injunctive relief. These pre-stipulations can limit your ability to contest an injunction application.

**The practical risk:** A counterparty with a dubious breach claim can get a TRO against you more easily if you've already conceded that any breach is irreparably harmful.

## The Takeaway

No NDA is truly "standard." The five provisions above appear routinely in agreements sent by companies that know exactly what they're doing. A 10-minute AI review or a 30-minute attorney scan catches all of them — before you're bound.

The cost of review is always less than the cost of a bad clause you didn't catch.
